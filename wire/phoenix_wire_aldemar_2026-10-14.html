The taperanked by how unusual
Sigma (σ) measures a move against that instrument’s own recent behaviour, not against zero. One sigma is a normal day. Two sigma is a move bigger than roughly 95% of its last twenty sessions — rare enough to be information rather than noise. A 1.8% day on the S&P is ordinary in a volatile month and remarkable in a calm one; sigma is what tells the difference.

The war · the dayWTI $95.09 · Aramco's headquarters hit

A Houthi missile hit the building that runs Saudi oil, and the price rose one percent

A Houthi missile struck an office tower inside Saudi Aramco's headquarters complex at Dhahran. A company source told Reuters the top three floors were damaged; the Saudi energy ministry reported a limited fire and no injuries. The site houses a major operations centre overseeing Aramco's onshore and offshore rigs, pipelines, refineries and export terminals — but the building hit was a command centre, not a producing or exporting asset. The Houthis said they launched ballistic missiles, cruise missiles and drones at Aramco facilities including Dhahran; only the Dhahran strike is confirmed. WTI settled $95.09, up 1.01%; Brent about $106.06, up 0.35%.

One percent is the number to think about. The nerve centre of the largest oil company on earth was hit by a missile and the price moved about a dollar. That is a market distinguishing precisely between damage to barrels and damage to buildings, and pricing only the first. It is also a market that has absorbed four weeks of this: strikes on Ukrainian energy infrastructure, tankers attacked in the Strait of Hormuz, Sanaa bombed, and now Dhahran. Each one has moved crude between nothing and three percent. None has moved it ten.

The IEA published the number that should worry anyone who buys diesel, however. Combined net gasoil exports from the Middle East and Russia fell 1.3m barrels a day in September, to 820,000 — down more than 60% year on year. The agency expects gasoil back in surplus in Q1 2027, but not by enough to offset the 2026 deficit, so stocks are unlikely to rebuild before the second half of 2027. Russia easing its diesel export restrictions will not fix it, because the binding constraint is how much Russian refining capacity is actually operating. The dislocation is visible in the prices that do not make headlines: dated Brent around $134 against futures near $107, other North Sea grades as high as $160, and wholesale diesel which topped $200 a barrel in Singapore in mid-September and about $220 in Europe and the United States.

And this is where the war meets the print. Gasoline did over half of September's CPI increase. Diesel is the input to freight, which is the input to the price of everything that moves. Gulf crude exports are back to 13.9m barrels a day, over 75% of pre-war levels, but refined product exports are 1.9m against 4.7m before the war — the crude is flowing and the refined product is not. That gap is the mechanism by which a conflict four thousand miles away sets the number that moved every position in this book on Wednesday. It is also why the October print, due 10 November, has the same risk built into it.
