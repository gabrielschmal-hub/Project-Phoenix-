The taperanked by how unusual
Sigma (σ) measures a move against that instrument’s own recent behaviour, not against zero. One sigma is a normal day. Two sigma is a move bigger than roughly 95% of its last twenty sessions — rare enough to be information rather than noise. A 1.8% day on the S&P is ordinary in a volatile month and remarkable in a calm one; sigma is what tells the difference.

The war · the daya hurricane replaces the war premium · crude $90.12

Twenty-five percent of Gulf output is shut in, and not because of Iran

Oil rose on weather. WTI gained 2.68% to $90.12 and Brent moved toward $102 with about a quarter of Gulf of Mexico oil output shut in ahead of Tropical Storm Isaias, which is forecast to reach the US Gulf coast as a hurricane early Saturday. Chevron, Shell and BP have pulled workers from platforms; Shell declared evacuation from six. This is the first session in a month where the marginal driver of the crude price had nothing to do with the Strait of Hormuz.

The war's own supply story has gone the other way. Kpler shipping data had regional crude exports above pre-conflict levels on 14 days in September, with the seven-day average at 18.3 million barrels a day on 30 September; OPEC+ held quotas at the weekend and Saudi Arabia cut Arab Light for Asia to $5 below benchmark, a six-year low. Against that, Aramco's Amin Nasser put commercial inventory at under 6 billion barrels against roughly 10 billion before the crisis, with less than 10% practically available and rebuilding at up to two years.

Which is exactly why a hurricane moved the price 2.7%. A market running at pre-war flow rates with a tenth of its usable inventory has no shock absorber, so any interruption — a storm, a strike on Yanbu, a collapse in the Qatari channel — prices immediately and hard. The diplomatic track has produced nothing since Washington's reply reached Tehran on 30 September. The G7's 100 million barrels, roughly a tenth of what has already been drawn, does not change the arithmetic.

For the book the read is unchanged and now better evidenced. At $90 crude is not the thing driving the long end — Wednesday's yield move came from sovereign debt in three continents, not from energy. But the asymmetry has sharpened: a market with no inventory buffer and a curve already at 24-year highs would reprice both in the same direction on a single supply event. Nothing in this book is positioned for that, and nothing needs to be while the nearest stop is nine percent away.
