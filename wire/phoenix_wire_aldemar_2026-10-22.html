The taperanked by how unusual
Sigma (σ) measures a move against that instrument’s own recent behaviour, not against zero. One sigma is a normal day. Two sigma is a move bigger than roughly 95% of its last twenty sessions — rare enough to be information rather than noise. A 1.8% day on the S&P is ordinary in a volatile month and remarkable in a calm one; sigma is what tells the difference.

What actually matteredone session, filtered

Wednesday's answer lasted nineteen hours

1. Meta's filing, not Meta's earnings, broke the AI trade. On Wednesday night the numbers looked like a vindication: revenue $64.60bn, up 26%, the 2026 capex floor raised to $135bn, and the shares up about 3% after hours. On Thursday a filing showed future commitments — mostly computing power and data-centre leases — well above their July level, total expense guidance went to $175-179bn, and the stock fell 6.98% to $648.85. Nothing in the results changed. What changed is that the market read the balance-sheet commitment rather than the income statement, and decided that $34.9bn of quarterly capex producing negative $2.12bn of free cash flow is a liability and not an investment. Meta ended the quarter with $82.4bn of cash against $83.7bn of debt, and the chief financial officer said there were no financing plans to announce.

2. And the distinction the market drew is the one Alphabet has to answer on the 28th. Meta funds its data centres out of advertising. Alphabet and Microsoft also rent capacity to outside customers, which is the structural defence: the spending is supposed to arrive back as cloud revenue. Except that on Wednesday Pichai said that cloud revenue is being deliberately held back to give DeepMind capacity, with new customers next year incredibly difficult. So the defence has been qualified by the company that owns it, six days before it reports. Alphabet fell 3.08% and Microsoft 1.5% while the semiconductor index fell more than 6% and Lam Research 9% on weak guidance. The chain is coherent: if the hyperscalers' spending is a liability, the people selling them equipment have a demand problem.

3. The bond market took the other side, and France went past the United States. The ten-year fell about 18 basis points to 4.95%, its largest one-day drop since August 2025, with the two-year down about 16 and the thirty-year 13. Hike odds for next week fell to about 10.5%. That is not an inflation trade — crude rose 2.4% on the same afternoon — it is money leaving equity risk and a growth scare being priced, with jobless claims at 194,000, a three-month low, ruling out the labour market as the cause. Meanwhile the French ten-year rose 6bp to 5.06%, a 24-year high, and crossed above the US ten-year for the first time since 2013. This brief spent Monday to Wednesday establishing that French politics does not move American yields. Thursday confirmed it from the opposite direction: the two curves moved apart as far as they have in thirteen years, in a single session, for entirely unrelated reasons.

The war · the dayBrent $113.17 · WTI $99.47

The strikes now have a date, and it is after the midterms

The New York Times reported that the United States and Israel have drawn up plans for renewed strikes on Iran after the 3 November midterms. The reported target set is energy sites, nuclear facilities, and missile and drone arsenals, with the report allowing a small chance that it happens this month. Trump said the US is not negotiating with Iran and that something will happen fairly soon; a separate report had the Israeli chief of staff seeing no operational need to strike before the election. That is the first time this conflict has had a date attached to it, and a dated war is a different instrument from an open-ended one — it gives the oil curve something to price rather than something to fear.

Crude priced it immediately. Brent rose $4.35, or 4%, to $113.17 and WTI $2.32, or 2.39%, to $99.47, a five-month high, after touching $100. The other half of the move was physical: an attack on King Fahd International Airport at Dammam killed one resident and injured eight, per Saudi Arabia's civil aviation authority, and there were further reported strikes on Saudi energy targets. The IRGC Navy said four oil-carrying vessels were hit and caught fire in the northern Persian Gulf, and three more struck mines west of the Strait of Hormuz, and warned that continued US escorting of ships through the Strait could bring responses against American regional bases.

And then the strangest line of the day. Trump accused Iran of making up fake hits on ships to drive up oil prices ahead of the midterms. Take it seriously as market information rather than as rhetoric: it is an American president publicly asserting that part of the war premium in the oil price is fabricated, eight days after signing an order waiving sanctions on Russian energy to hold fuel prices down, and twelve days before an election. Whether or not the claim is true, it tells you the energy line in the inflation data is now being managed as a political variable, and that the administration's tolerance for a hundred-dollar barrel before 3 November is low.

For this book the transmission has changed shape. A fortnight ago crude rising meant yields rising, and that was the whole mechanism — energy into inflation into the long end into everything. On Thursday crude rose 2.4% and the ten-year fell 18 basis points. The bond market has stopped treating the oil shock as an inflation problem and started treating it as a demand one, which is the same conclusion the housing data forced on Tuesday. Gold fell 2.81% on the same afternoon, which only makes sense if the market is pricing growth damage rather than price pressure. If that holds, the energy premium stops being the thing that threatens this book's valuations and becomes the thing that threatens its earnings.
