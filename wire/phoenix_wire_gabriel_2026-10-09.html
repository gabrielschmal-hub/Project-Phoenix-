The taperanked by how unusual
Sigma (σ) measures a move against that instrument’s own recent behaviour, not against zero. One sigma is a normal day. Two sigma is a move bigger than roughly 95% of its last twenty sessions — rare enough to be information rather than noise. A 1.8% day on the S&P is ordinary in a volatile month and remarkable in a calm one; sigma is what tells the difference.

The war · the weekcrude $90.76 · no strike before November

The week the war stopped setting the oil price

Crude ended the week almost exactly where it started. WTI settled $90.76, down 0.80% on Friday and $91.11 to $90.76 across the week — a move of less than four tenths of a percent over five sessions that contained a hurricane, a tanker attack and a presidential commitment on military timing. Brent finished $103.35. The range within that was wide: $87.77 on Tuesday, almost $93 on Thursday.

The single most consequential thing said was about the calendar. On Thursday Trump said the United States will not attack Iran before the November midterm elections, citing productive diplomacy. That is the first dated commitment on timing since the conflict began, and it removes the tail the oil price had been intermittently pricing since late September — for about four weeks. Iranian attacks on tankers in the Strait of Hormuz happened the same day, which is the pattern of the whole fortnight: a channel that functions and a military situation that keeps producing incidents.

What actually moved the price was weather and supply. Hurricane Isaias shut in about a quarter of Gulf of Mexico output and sent Chevron, Shell and BP to evacuate platforms; by Friday the premium was coming out as the path clarified. On the other side, OPEC+ held quotas, Saudi Arabia cut Arab Light for Asia to $5 below benchmark, a six-year low, and Kpler had regional exports above pre-conflict levels on 14 days in September, with a seven-day average of 18.3m barrels a day on the 30th. The flow is back.

The buffer is not, and that is the part to carry forward. Aramco's Amin Nasser put commercial inventory at under 6 billion barrels against roughly 10 billion before the crisis, less than 10% practically available, with rebuilding at up to two years even after Hormuz fully reopens. A market at pre-war flow rates with a tenth of its usable stock prices continuity and cannot absorb interruption — which is why a storm moved crude 2.7% on Wednesday. Nothing in this book is positioned for an oil shock and nothing needs to be, but a renewed disruption would move crude and the curve the same way at once.
