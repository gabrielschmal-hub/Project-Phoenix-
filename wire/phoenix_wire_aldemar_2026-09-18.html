The taperanked by how unusual
Sigma (σ) measures a move against that instrument’s own recent behaviour, not against zero. One sigma is a normal day. Two sigma is a move bigger than roughly 95% of its last twenty sessions — rare enough to be information rather than noise. A 1.8% day on the S&P is ordinary in a volatile month and remarkable in a calm one; sigma is what tells the difference.

Seven trillion dollars expired and the largest move on the board was 0.64 sigma. The Russell's 0.50% fall led, the Nasdaq's 0.39% gain came second at 0.49σ and the S&P's 0.17% was 0.29σ — the quietest session of a week that contained two central-bank decisions and the second-largest options expiry ever recorded. That is worth sitting with. The expiry did not produce a move because expiries do not produce moves on their own: they amplify whatever direction is already there, and by Friday there was none.

The indices. The S&P closed 7,650.50, the Nasdaq 26,522.55, the Dow 51,682.64 and the Russell 2,876.85. Technology and industrials advanced while small caps lagged for a second day — the same pattern as Wednesday and the opposite of Thursday, which is what a market with no view looks like when it is being pushed around by strike positioning rather than by information.

Rates gave Thursday back. The ten-year rose 5.3bp to 5.00%, returning to the line it had slipped under, and the two-year rose 6.6bp to 4.756%, a fresh 52-week high. The front end leading is the part that matters: the market is not repricing term premium, it is repricing how many more times this Fed moves. Thursday's read — that the hike bought credibility — survived one session before the curve went back to pricing the dot plot.

The yen did the opposite of what a hike implies. USD/JPY above 157 after the Bank of Japan raised to 1.25%, because a 7-2 split and a governor who would not commit to a pace told the market that follow-up hikes are not imminent. The US-Japan rate gap is therefore wider, not narrower, and the carry trade the whole market has been watching for signs of unwinding got paid instead. Treasury Secretary Bessent had publicly urged the BoJ to tighten faster and prevent yen depreciation; he got the hike and not the currency.

Commodities. Crude fell a fourth straight session, 1.19% to $100.70, now within a dollar of the $100 line it broke through when the pipeline was cut. Gold rose 0.49% to $4,421.30. Volumes across the book were two to three times normal on the expiry — AAPL traded 85.9m shares against a typical 35m — which is mechanical, not directional.

What actually matteredone session, filtered

A central bank hiked and its currency fell — the one outcome nobody was hedged for

1. The Bank of Japan delivered a dovish hike, which is the awkward combination. It raised to 1.25%, the highest since 1995 and the sixth increase since leaving negative rates in March 2024 — and the yen weakened past 157. The mechanism was the vote: 7-2, with Asada and Sato preferring to hold, plus a governor who answered questions about pace by saying nothing should be ruled out. A split board is a signal that the next hike is not imminent, and the market priced the pace rather than the level. The US-Japan gap is wider this morning than it was on Thursday.

2. That matters more than it looks, because the carry trade is now larger, not smaller. Every brief this month has watched for a disorderly unwind of the $764bn to $1.1tn funded in yen, on the theory that a BoJ hike forces it. The hike came and the trade got paid. The risk has not been discharged — it has been deferred and enlarged, and the condition this brief has carried for weeks (BoJ hikes and USD/JPY breaks below 150) is now further from being met than at any point since it was written. Treasury Secretary Bessent had pressed publicly for faster tightening; the currency ignored both him and the central bank.

3. Seven trillion dollars expired and nothing happened, which is the useful fact. The second-largest expiry on record produced the calmest session of the week. Expiries do not create direction, they magnify whatever is already present — and after a Fed hike, a BoJ hike and a round trip through five percent on the ten-year, the market arrived at Friday with no view left to magnify. Positioning is now cleaner than it has been all quarter, which makes next week's tape a better signal than this week's.

The war · the daycrude's fourth straight fall · back to $100

The supply shock has almost completely deflated

Crude fell a fourth consecutive session, 1.19% to $100.70, and is now within a dollar of the line it broke through when the East-West pipeline was cut on 13 September. From $104.10 on Tuesday to $100.70 on Friday is a 3.3% retreat across four days, driven entirely by Saudi Aramco's repair plan: bypass the damaged section, restore roughly half the capacity within days, full operations in about six weeks, and move barrels to Asian refiners ship-to-ship near Oman's Sohar port in the meantime.

The structural picture is unchanged — Hormuz flows remain near 6-9m bpd against more than a fifth of world supply in peacetime — but the panic premium has gone. That matters for one reason above all: the dot plot published on Wednesday was written while crude was still rising. Petrol added 3.9% in August and carried the CPI headline to 3.4%, which took hike odds from 70% to above 90%. If this retreat holds into the October print, the projection that twelve of eighteen members signed is the first thing that has to move.
