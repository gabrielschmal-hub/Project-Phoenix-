The week two central banks tightened — and the S&P 500 finished flat
Five sessions containing the first Fed rate rise since 2023, the Bank of Japan’s move to a 31-year high, a ten-year that touched 5.045% and about $7 trillion of options expiry — and the S&P 500 ended down 0.08%. The damage was concentrated and specific: the Dow fell 1.69% while the Nasdaq rose 0.72%, because a Fed promising to hold rates higher marks down the economy rather than the multiple. The BoJ hiked on a 7-2 split and the yen weakened past 157, so the carry trade got paid rather than unwound. In the book BAC closed at its original stop on Monday for −$23.00 after Moynihan guided fees down at least 10%, OKTA was opened the same afternoon and round-tripped to finish 41 cents below its fill, and nothing else moved. The S&P 500 ended the week down 0.08%. Six one-hundredths of a percent, across five sessions that contained the first Federal Reserve rate rise since 2023, the Bank of Japan's sixth hike since leaving negative rates, a ten-year Treasury yield that touched 5.045% and the second-largest options expiry on record. That is the single most useful number in this brief: an enormous amount happened and the index did not move.

Underneath it, one thing did. The Dow fell 1.69%, a 1.41 sigma week and the only instrument on the board to travel meaningfully outside its ordinary range. The Russell fell 0.93%, gold rose 0.28% and crude added 0.65% after a round trip to $104 and back. The Nasdaq rose 0.72%. A week that hurt the old economy and left the new one alone.

The curve did the real work. The ten-year began the week at 4.94%, closed above 5.006% on Tuesday for the first time since 2007, held it through the Fed decision, fell back under it on Thursday when the bond market decided the hike had bought credibility, and returned to 5.00% on Friday. The two-year ended at 4.756%, a fresh 52-week high, and the thirty-year reached 5.369%. The front end leading all week means the market is repricing how many more times this Fed moves, not the term premium.

And the yen went the wrong way. USD/JPY finished above 157 after the Bank of Japan raised rates on Friday — weaker, not stronger, on a hike. The carry trade everyone has been waiting to see unwind was paid instead.

The week cost this book −$23.00 — one position, BAC, closed at its original stop on Monday for a clean −1.00R. Nothing was widened, averaged down or talked out of. Heat is 3.00R against a 3.0R cap, and it did not change once across five sessions, because after Monday nothing was added, trimmed or moved. In a week with two central bank decisions in it, that is the whole of the performance.

BAC was killed by a fee line, not by the macro. Brian Moynihan told the Barclays conference on Monday that third-quarter investment banking fees would fall at least 10% with trading flat. The stock fell 5.14% to 59.47 on 86% above-average volume, low of day 58.92. JPMorgan fell 1.71% and Wells 1.75% on the same headline — three times the sector move, so this was a single-name repricing. The position was held for higher-for-longer rates; the ten-year touched five percent that same day; it died anyway.

Seven cents decided this book's best performer. PLTR's low on Thursday 10 September was 164.55. This stop sits at 164.45 — it survived by ten cents, while the other account's 164.62 did not. The position finished this week up 6.2%, the strongest in either book, and is now 7.43% clear of that same stop against 0.75% when the week began. Nothing about that outcome was skill.

OKTA was entered on Monday and ended the week where it started. The fill at 182.73 came after the stock had already risen 9.75% that session on the cybersecurity bid — it closed Monday at 186.45, ran to 190.45 by Tuesday, and then gave all of it back on Friday's expiry, closing 182.32. Down 41 cents on the week against a stop that was never within 11% of being touched. A full round trip that cost nothing is what a correctly-placed stop buys you.

What actually matteredfive sessions, filtered

Two central banks tightened and the only thing that repriced was the economy trade

1. The Fed hiked 25bp to 3.75-4.00% on Wednesday, unanimously. The first increase since 2023, and the statement dropped the three dissents it had carried because it gave them what they wanted. The language was unusually blunt — the plain fact is that inflation is too high and has been for too long — and the dot plot has twelve of eighteen members projecting one more hike this year. Stocks were higher at 2pm and fell through Warsh's press conference: the Dow dropped more than 700 points while he spoke.

2. The damage landed on cyclicals, not on duration. Wednesday's split — Dow −1.15%, Nasdaq flat — and the week's — Dow −1.69%, Nasdaq +0.72% — tell the same story. A central bank that hikes and signals more should punish long-duration growth first. It did the opposite, because Warsh saying the economy has strengthened while promising to hold rates higher is, to a cyclical business, a promise to slow it down. This book has almost no exposure to that trade, which is why a hawkish week cost it nothing.

3. The Bank of Japan delivered a dovish hike, which is the awkward combination. It raised to 1.25% on Friday, the highest since 1995 — and the yen weakened past 157. The mechanism was the vote: 7-2, with two members preferring to hold, plus a governor who would not commit to a pace. A split board says the next hike is not imminent, so the market priced the pace rather than the level. The $764bn to $1.1tn funded in yen did not unwind; it got paid. The condition this brief has carried for weeks — BoJ hikes and USD/JPY breaks below 150 — is further from being met than at any point since it was written.

4. And the AI-safety trade turned out to be conditional, not structural. Monday's essay from Anthropic's Dario Amodei moved three names in this book the same way on the same day. By Wednesday they had decoupled entirely — PLTR +1.03%, OKTA −1.22%, GOOG −0.61%. The correlation is real on story-days and absent otherwise, which makes it a risk that appears precisely when it matters and is invisible the rest of the time. No screener would flag it, because the names sit in different industries.

The war · the weekcrude round-tripped to $104 and back

The supply shock got a repair timeline and deflated in four sessions

The week opened with the Saudi East-West pipeline shut — suspended on 13 September after a drone strike traced to Maysan province in south-eastern Iraq. The 1,200km line normally carries 4-5m barrels a day to Yanbu on the Red Sea and exists precisely to bypass the Strait of Hormuz, where flows are already down to roughly 6-9m bpd from over a fifth of world supply. Crude ran to $104.10 by Tuesday and Brent above $107.

Then Aramco said it could bypass the damaged section and restore about half the capacity within days, with full operations in roughly six weeks, while moving barrels to Asian refiners ship-to-ship near Oman's Sohar port. US commercial inventories fell only 640,000 barrels against an expected 1.6m draw. Crude then fell four sessions running, to $100.70 on Friday — up just 0.65% on the week after all of it.

This is the input that matters most for what comes next. Petrol added 3.9% in August and carried the CPI headline to 3.4%, which took September hike odds from roughly 70% to above 90%. The dot plot twelve members signed on Wednesday was written while crude was still rising. If this retreat holds into the October print, that projection is the first thing that has to move.

After that, nothing until late October. Alphabet reports 28 October, the same day as the next Fed decision; Apple 29 October; Palantir 2 November. Five weeks with no earnings risk in the book — and equally, nothing arriving to rescue a position with a number.

Three things to watch. Whether the yen keeps weakening now the BoJ has shown its hand, because a carry trade that survives its own catalyst gets larger before it breaks. Whether crude holds under $100, the one input that can soften the next hike. And whether the Dow's underperformance persists — one week of cyclicals lagging is rotation, a month is a growth scare, and this book is positioned for the first and not the second.
