The taperanked by how unusual
Sigma (σ) measures a move against that instrument’s own recent behaviour, not against zero. One sigma is a normal day. Two sigma is a move bigger than roughly 95% of its last twenty sessions — rare enough to be information rather than noise. A 1.8% day on the S&P is ordinary in a volatile month and remarkable in a calm one; sigma is what tells the difference.

Gulf flows fell from twenty million barrels a day to eight and a half

That is the number. Gulf crude flows fell to a seven-day average of about 8.5m barrels a day, down from over 20m the previous week, after at least ten incidents involving commercial vessels. On Sunday Iran's Revolutionary Guards said an oil tanker exploded after hitting a naval mine in the southern Strait of Hormuz. A fortnight ago this brief was reporting Gulf exports at 13.9m barrels a day, over 75% of pre-war levels, and Kpler warning that the recovery would be uneven. It has not been uneven; it has reversed.

And the targets widened again. The Houthis claimed strikes on an Aramco facility at Yanbu — the Red Sea terminal whose purpose is to bypass Hormuz, and the same port a Saudi-led coalition intercepted a ballistic missile over on Friday — along with the Shaybah oilfield and airports at Riyadh and Abha. Saudi authorities have not confirmed any of it and none of it has been independently verified, so it is a claim rather than a fact. It followed an attack in Yemen's Shabwa province that killed 53 people. Whether or not the strikes landed, a campaign that now names the capital's airport and the hedge against the chokepoint is a different campaign from the one that was attacking tankers three weeks ago.

The offset is political and explicitly dated. Trump signed an executive order waiving certain sanctions and tariffs on Russian energy exports. Analysts read it as an attempt to ease fuel prices before the 3 November midterms; implementation is uncertain. It is the mirror image of the IEA and reserve releases that failed to hold the price down on Friday — a supply-side intervention aimed at the pump rather than at the barrel — and it is the clearest statement yet of how much the energy line in the inflation data now matters politically. Brent took it as a reason to sell: $109.55, down 31 cents, on profit-taking after a five-month high.

WTI went the other way, and the spread is the thing to watch now. WTI settled $103.66, up $2.25 or 2.2%, its highest settlement since 15 September, so the gap to Brent collapsed to $5.89 from $8.45 — and from $12.91 a week ago. The American barrel is tightening against the world's at speed, which is a function of the 8.6m-barrel US inventory draw reported on Friday rather than of anything in the Gulf. For this book the transmission is unchanged and getting stronger: gasoline did over half of September's CPI increase, crude has gone from $90.76 to $103.66 in six sessions, and the October print lands 10 November.
