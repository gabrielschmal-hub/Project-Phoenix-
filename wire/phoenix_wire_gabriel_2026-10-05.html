The taperanked by how unusual
Sigma (σ) measures a move against that instrument’s own recent behaviour, not against zero. One sigma is a normal day. Two sigma is a move bigger than roughly 95% of its last twenty sessions — rare enough to be information rather than noise. A 1.8% day on the S&P is ordinary in a volatile month and remarkable in a calm one; sigma is what tells the difference.

The war · the daySaudi cuts to a six-year low · crude $90.68

The man selling the oil says there is less than ten percent of it available

Two supply decisions landed over the weekend. Major OPEC+ members agreed to leave production quotas unchanged for next month, in line with the existing output roadmap. And Saudi Aramco cut the official selling price of Arab Light for Asian buyers to $5 a barrel below the regional benchmark for November — a six-year low, from a $2 discount in October. The reason given was market share: Gulf supply has become more available and Hormuz flows have improved.

And then the Aramco chief executive described the state of global inventory. Speaking at the Energy Intelligence Forum in London on Monday, Amin Nasser called stockpiles scarily thin and said less than 10% of the world's oil inventories are practically available. His figures: roughly 10 billion barrels held when the crisis began against under 6 billion of commercial inventory today; about 3 billion barrels of supply lost since the Iran war started; over 1 billion barrels released from stockpiles to offset it; and an extra 2 million barrels a day needed for eighteen months to rebuild. Rebuilding would take up to two years even after the Strait of Hormuz fully reopens.

Set the G7's Friday announcement against those numbers. 100 million barrels over four months is roughly a tenth of what has already been drawn from stockpiles and about a thirtieth of the supply lost. It will do something for diesel sentiment ahead of winter — that was the point, and officials said as much when they cautioned that strategic crude does not become diesel without refinery capacity. It does not rebuild a buffer.

For the book the implication is a specific asymmetry. WTI fell 0.47% to $90.68 and Brent rose to $102.90 after touching $103. At that level crude is neither pushing the long end up nor pulling it down, so the rate problem still has to be explained by prices paid, auction supply and term premium. But a market with under 10% of its inventory practically available prices the next disruption differently from one with a buffer — and the diplomatic channel has produced nothing since Washington's reply reached Tehran via Qatar on 30 September.
