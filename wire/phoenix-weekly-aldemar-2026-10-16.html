Two hot inflation prints and the long end fell through both of them. September CPI came in +0.6% on the month and +3.7% on the year and September PPI at +1.3% against +0.5% expected — yet the ten-year finished the week about 14 basis points lower and the thirty-year 17 lower at 5.43%, with hike odds for 28 October ending near 27% after touching 50%. The Nasdaq fell 4.48% at 1.99σ, crude rose 11.73% at 1.85σ and the S&P fell 1.95% at 1.69σ. And then the two that did nothing: the Dow rose 0.67% at 0.63σ and gold fell 0.20% at 0.13σ. A week in which the small-cap index has its most unusual move of the series, the Nasdaq has its worst week since June, crude rises eleven percent and gold does not move at all is not a week with one story in it.

The indices, precisely. Across the five sessions the S&P fell 152.10 points, or 1.9%, to 7,659.44; the Nasdaq 1,226.14, or 4.5%, to 26,140.03 — its worst week since June, as was the S&P's; the Russell 85.61, or 3%, to 2,721.37. The Dow rose 347.46 points, or 0.7%, to 52,002.41, and was the only index up. For the year the S&P is now up 11.9%, the Nasdaq 12.5%, the Russell 9.6% and the Dow 8.2% — the Dow has closed almost the whole of the gap the Nasdaq built over the first nine months.

Two first-tier prints, both hot, and the long end fell anyway. September CPI on Wednesday came in +0.6% on the month and +3.7% on the year, core +0.3% and +2.6%, both above forecast — with gasoline +9.1% and +34.2% doing over half the monthly increase on the BLS's own reckoning, and shelter and core services both accelerating to +0.4%. September PPI on Thursday came in at +1.3% against +0.5% expected, +6.3% on the year, with energy goods +10.8% and gasoline +20.3% at the producer level. Hike odds for 28 October went from about 22% to roughly 50% to about 27% inside three sessions. And the ten-year finished the week about 14 basis points lower and the thirty-year about 17, at 5.43%. Fed Chair Warsh's IMF remarks left roughly an 85% hold priced for this month.

The number that explains it is in the retail sales report, not the inflation ones. Headline retail sales rose 0.8% against 0.3% expected, to $745.0bn. But the control group rose only 0.1% against 0.3% expected, and excluding petrol stations the increase was 0.2%. So the consumer is not spending more; the consumer is paying more for fuel. Gasoline did over half of CPI, rose a fifth at the producer level, and supplied most of the retail beat. The bond market read that as a tax on demand rather than inflation to be met with hikes, and bought duration into two hot prints. That is the single most important thing the week established.

And crude broke a hundred dollars. WTI went from $90.76 to $101.41, +11.7%, and Brent from $103.35 to $109.86, touching $110 on Friday for the first time since May. Gold finished the week almost exactly where it started, at $4,166.39, having fallen 1.34% on the CPI and risen 1.60% on the PPI. An eleven-percent week in oil and a flat week in gold is a market pricing a supply problem, not a monetary one.

What actually matteredfive sessions, filtered

Two hot prints, a bank fine, a foldable sell-out, and a rotation that completed

1. The long end fell through two hot inflation prints, and that settles a three-week argument. The thesis built from 29 September was that the long end was rising on supply and global debt rather than inflation. This week tested it to destruction: the ten-year spiked to 5.33% on the CPI and closed back at 5.19%; then fell 12 basis points on the PPI to a three-week low; and finished the week about 14 basis points lower, with the thirty-year 17 lower at 5.43%. Neither explanation survives. An inflation trade would have risen and stayed risen. A pure supply trade would not have spiked on CPI at all. What fits is the retail sales control group at +0.1%: the market read energy-driven price rises as demand destruction and bought duration into them. Hike odds for 28 October ended the week near 27% after touching 50%.

2. The banks reported and the split told you where the cycle is. JPMorgan missed for the first time in nearly three years — $5.57 against $5.93 expected — on investment banking and a fixed-income desk that slowed from the summer. Goldman earned $17.01 against $12.25 a year ago on stock trading, with bond trading slower. Citigroup's profit rose 40% but card profits were flat. Bank of America missed on Wednesday — $0.91 a share including a charge, $1.08 against $1.10 excluding it, revenue $30bn against $30.58bn, investment banking fees −11% and sales and trading −5% — the day after the Treasury fined it $1.2bn, the third-largest penalty in that department's history, for willful Bank Secrecy Act violations involving more than $5bn of suspicious transactions. The pattern: equity desks busy, bond desks quiet, consumer flat.

3. Apple's foldable sold out in minutes, which was the only checkable fact in a fortnight of sourcing. Three parties had disagreed from anonymous supply-chain contacts — Nikkei on iPhone 18 Pro cuts, Securities Times on Duo orders raised 30% toward ten million units, Bank of America on shorter Pro delivery times — and Friday replaced all of it with evidence. Pre-orders opened at 8am Eastern, stock went within moments, shipping slipped to late January 2027 on some configurations, Apple called demand nothing short of extraordinary, and units resold at up to $5,000 against a $1,999 list. The fair caveat is that a sell-out is a ratio and Apple probably built few of them. The resale premium is harder to argue with.

4. And the rotation completed, which is the week's real lesson for this book. Over five sessions the Dow rose 0.7% and was the only index up, while the Nasdaq fell 4.5% and the Russell 3%. Friday's sell-off is the one to study: the Nasdaq fell 2.15% and the Dow 0.34% while the ten-year rose two basis points. That was not a rate event. Micron fell 5.6% and Intel 5.8% as investors grew more concerned about how long AI infrastructure spending will last, and Okta, Palantir, Datadog, Zscaler and Elastic were sold together. A rate scare reverses when the Fed is repriced — and it did, on Thursday. A thesis under question does not.

The war · the weekWTI $90.76 → $101.41 · Brent touched $110

Crude rose eleven percent through two emergency releases

WTI went from $90.76 to $101.41 across the week, up 11.7%, and Brent touched $110 on Friday for the first time since May. It did so through four separate supply events and against the two largest tools short of policy that the agencies have. Monday: +3.28% on renewed Russian strikes on Ukrainian energy infrastructure and tanker attacks in the Strait of Hormuz. Tuesday: WTI up, Brent down, as traders weighed a possible US–Russia diesel deal against escalation in Yemen. Wednesday: +1.01% after a Houthi missile hit an office tower at Aramco's Dhahran headquarters — top three floors damaged, limited fire, no injuries, a command centre rather than a producing asset. Thursday: +3.03% on a US inventory draw. Friday: +3.51%.

Friday's reasons are worth separating because one of them is new. A Saudi-led coalition intercepted a ballistic missile aimed at Yanbu — the Red Sea export terminal whose entire strategic value is that crude leaving from it bypasses the Strait of Hormuz. Targeting the hedge against the chokepoint is an escalation in kind, not degree. Ukrainian strikes also damaged the Ryazan refinery, shutting a primary crude unit, which bears directly on Russian diesel. And the EIA reported US crude stocks down 8.6m barrels to 415.6m, with distillates down 2.5m.

Against all of which: the IEA announced the release of over 20m barrels of emergency supply, and Washington offered up to 17m through a reserve exchange. Roughly 37m barrels of announced additional supply, and the price rose 3.5% on the day and 11.7% on the week. The reason is the number Aramco's Amin Nasser has been repeating: commercial inventory under 6 billion barrels against roughly 10 billion before the crisis, with less than 10% practically available and up to two years to rebuild. A release needs a cushion to land on. There is not one.

And the diesel number is how all of this reaches a book with no oil position in it. The IEA reported combined net gasoil exports from the Middle East and Russia down 1.3m barrels a day in September to 820,000 — more than 60% lower year on year — with no stock rebuild expected before the second half of 2027, because the binding constraint is how much Russian refining capacity is actually running. Gulf crude exports are back at 13.9m barrels a day, over 75% of pre-war; refined product exports are 1.9m against 4.7m. Crude flows and product does not. That gap is why gasoline rose 20.3% at the producer level in September and did over half of the CPI increase — and the October print, due 10 November, carries a crude price eleven percent higher than when this week began.
