Three index records were set this week and the only index that fell was the one measuring the median company. The Russell lost 0.91% — 2.12σ, the largest reading on the board — while the S&P rose 1.15% at 1.69σ, the Dow 0.93% and the Nasdaq 0.64% at 1.01σ. In point terms: S&P +88.82, Dow +477.99, Nasdaq +175.31, Russell −25.91. A record-setting week in which small caps fell is not a rally with poor breadth; it is two different markets, and this is the third consecutive week the gap has widened.

The rotation inverted, and the week is the evidence. The Dow beat the Nasdaq over the five sessions — 0.93% against 0.64% — after a quarter in which the Nasdaq beat the Dow by more than five points. The Dow led on three of the last four sessions. That is not a one-day funding effect any more. For a book holding three long-duration growth names and nothing on the other side, it is the single most relevant structural fact of the week, and it sits oddly beside the book's own result.

The rate story turned two-sided for the first time. Monday through Wednesday it ran exactly as it had for a fortnight: ISM services prices paid up 1.4 to 74.0 against 72.9 expected, a second month above 72, while employment sat at 50.1; the two-year falling on the weak half and the ten-year making 52-week highs at 5.315% and touching 5.35% intraday, the thirty-year at 5.670%. Wednesday added the global framing — France's index down 1.2% amid mass budget protests, debt heading for 121.7% of GDP in 2027, and projected interest payments of €74.5bn exceeding what the country spends on education. The IMF's Georgieva cited record public debt; Ray Dalio warned about America's.

Then it stopped behaving. Thursday the long end eased through a thirty-year auction — the first direct test of the supply argument, and the ten-year closed near 5.24%, its lowest in a week. Friday the curve flattened: the two-year rose 3.9 basis points to 4.795% while the thirty-year sat flat at 5.608%, and it did so on a Michigan sentiment print that fell to 46.3 from 48.1 against 47.6 expected. Two consecutive sessions now fail to fit the term-premium story after five that fitted it exactly. It is not refuted — the thirty-year is still near a 24-year high and the September Fed minutes promise another hike this year — but this is the first sustained counter-evidence, and CPI on Tuesday decides it.

And the week ended with a structural repricing nobody had on the calendar. A reported $8bn SpaceX purchase of 800 MHz spectrum for Starlink Mobile took 12.26% out of T-Mobile on Friday and about 10.9% each out of Verizon and AT&T, while Crown Castle rose 11.27% and the other tower owners with it. Communication Services closed the week's worst sector. Crude went almost nowhere across five sessions — $91.11 to $90.76 — having been $87.77 on Tuesday and almost $93 on Thursday. Gold rose 0.83% to $4,174.68, its first weekly gain in three.

The rate thesis acquired its first counter-evidence, and the rotation inverted

1. Two sessions broke the pattern that five had built. From 29 September the long end rose on data that argued for lower yields four times in five sessions — a twelve-year low in consumer confidence, a downside core PCE miss at 3.0% against 3.3%, payrolls at 29,000 against 90,000 with 60,000 revised out, and Monday's ISM services. By Wednesday the thirty-year was at 5.671% and the ten-year had touched 5.35%, its highest since 2002, with the framing turning global. Then Thursday the long end eased through a thirty-year auction, and Friday the curve flattened on a weak sentiment print. An auction is where a supply thesis is actually tested, and that one did not go badly. This brief has published the supply argument for a fortnight; it is recording the counter-evidence with the same weight.

2. The index records and the median stock are describing different economies. The S&P set its first record since 13 August on Tuesday with genuinely broad internals — ten of eleven sectors, the equal-weight index matching the cap-weighted one, positive volume at 65.1% of NYSE turnover — and still only 24 new 52-week highs, with the McClellan Summation negative. By Friday the week showed the S&P up 1.2% and the Russell down 0.9%, and Michigan consumer sentiment had fallen to 46.3. Three records in a week when small caps fell and the consumer reached a multi-decade low of confidence is a market carried by its largest weights. This book owns three of those weights, which is why it had its largest weekly gain in three weeks.

3. The AI trade showed its first crack, and it was about funding. Thursday the Nasdaq fell 1.25% on a semiconductor sell-off while the Dow rose and most S&P constituents were higher — the first session in this run where the AI complex moved alone. The trigger was an Nvidia-backed Australian data-centre company failing to raise the support it had sought: a question about the capital behind capacity rather than about demand for compute, and the more dangerous of the two, because capacity already contracted has to be paid for regardless. Alphabet carries $195-205bn of guided capex and, since Tuesday, a twenty-year obligation for 3.6 gigawatts of power. It was the only holding to fall that day. By Friday the market had moved on; the question has not been answered.

The war · the weekcrude −0.38% · no strike before November

The week the war stopped setting the price of oil

Crude ended the week where it started. WTI went $91.11 to $90.76, a move of less than four tenths of a percent across five sessions that contained a hurricane, a tanker attack and a presidential commitment on military timing. The range inside that was wide — $87.77 on Tuesday, almost $93 on Thursday — and Brent finished $103.35. A price that round-trips through that much news is a price that has stopped taking its direction from the conflict.

The most consequential thing said was about the calendar. On Thursday Trump said the United States will not attack Iran before the November midterm elections, citing productive diplomacy. It is the first dated commitment on timing since the conflict began and it removes, for roughly four weeks, the tail the oil price had been intermittently pricing. Iranian attacks on tankers in the Strait of Hormuz happened the same day. Washington's reply to Tehran's seven-point proposal reached the cabinet through Qatari mediation on 30 September and neither side has disclosed its contents since.

What moved the price was weather and Saudi pricing. Hurricane Isaias shut in about a quarter of Gulf of Mexico output mid-week, with Chevron, Shell and BP evacuating platforms and Shell pulling off six; the premium came out on Friday as the path clarified. Against that, OPEC+ held quotas at the previous weekend and Saudi Arabia cut Arab Light for Asian buyers to $5 below benchmark, a six-year low, from a $2 discount, to take share as Gulf flows recovered. Kpler had regional exports above pre-conflict levels on 14 days in September, with a seven-day average of 18.3m barrels a day on the 30th.

And the man selling the oil spent Monday explaining that there is none spare. Aramco's Amin Nasser, at the Energy Intelligence Forum in London, called global stockpiles scarily thin: under 6 billion barrels of commercial inventory against roughly 10 billion before the crisis, less than 10% practically available, around 3 billion barrels of supply lost and over a billion drawn to cover it, with rebuilding at up to two years even after Hormuz fully reopens and an extra 2 million barrels a day needed for eighteen months. The flow has recovered and the buffer has not. That is why a storm moved crude 2.7% on Wednesday, and it is the asymmetry this book carries without being positioned for it.
