The taperanked by how unusual
Sigma (σ) measures a move against that instrument’s own recent behaviour, not against zero. One sigma is a normal day. Two sigma is a move bigger than roughly 95% of its last twenty sessions — rare enough to be information rather than noise. A 1.8% day on the S&P is ordinary in a volatile month and remarkable in a calm one; sigma is what tells the difference.

The war · the daycrude $93.74 · premium back without a strike

Crude's largest gain in a fortnight, on supply rather than on Washington

WTI settled $93.74, up 3.28%, and Brent $106.65, up 3.19% — a spread of about $12.90, and crude's largest single-session gain since the start of the month. The move came from two places at once: renewed Russian strikes on Ukrainian energy infrastructure, which take refining and export capacity out rather than wellhead supply, and fresh attacks on tankers in the Strait of Hormuz, which take the shipping out. Both are interruptions to flow and neither requires anything new from the United States or Iran.

Set that against what was said last week. On Thursday Trump committed to no American attack on Iran before the November midterms, the first dated commitment on timing since the conflict began, and crude fell 0.80% on Friday. Two sessions later it is up more than three percent on a different mechanism entirely. The practical reading is that the strike tail was one input and the market has four weeks of relief on that one specific risk — not on the price.

Weather, separately. Hurricane Isaias made landfall on the Florida Panhandle, with at least four dead as it pushed inland. Last week it shut in roughly a quarter of Gulf of Mexico output and sent Chevron, Shell and BP to evacuate platforms, and the premium came out as its path clarified into Friday. A Panhandle landfall is east of most of the producing infrastructure, so the production story is largely over even as the human one is not.

And the structural point has not changed. Aramco's Amin Nasser puts commercial inventory under 6 billion barrels against roughly 10 billion before the crisis, with less than 10% practically available and rebuilding at up to two years even after Hormuz fully reopens. Regional flow is back at pre-conflict rates; the buffer behind it is not. That is precisely why two attacks and no change in policy move the price three percent. Nothing in this book is positioned for an oil shock and nothing needs to be — but a market that reprices this far on flow interruption will move the curve as well, and CPI is tomorrow.
