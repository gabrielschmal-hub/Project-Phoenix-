The taperanked by how unusual
Sigma (σ) measures a move against that instrument’s own recent behaviour, not against zero. One sigma is a normal day. Two sigma is a move bigger than roughly 95% of its last twenty sessions — rare enough to be information rather than noise. A 1.8% day on the S&P is ordinary in a volatile month and remarkable in a calm one; sigma is what tells the difference.

The record was set on a sub-sigma session, and that is the most interesting thing about it. The S&P's 0.58% was 0.91σ — the largest reading on the board and still inside an ordinary day. Crude's 2.68% fall was 0.81σ, the Russell's 0.59% decline 0.78σ, the Dow's 0.49% 0.78σ and the Nasdaq's 0.45% 0.52σ. Nothing cleared one. Markets that grind to records on sub-sigma days are doing something different from markets that gap to them: the move is being made by participation, not by force.

And the participation is measurable. Ten of eleven sectors advanced for a third consecutive session, utilities and real estate outperforming on the back of lower yields. The equal-weight S&P matched the cap-weighted index at +0.6%, which is the single cleanest refutation of the narrowness that carried Monday's Nasdaq record. Positive volume was 65.1% of NYSE turnover, the strongest since 2 September. This was the first session in two weeks where the index and its components agreed with each other.

Two things argue the repair is early rather than complete. New 52-week highs on the S&P numbered only 24 — a thin figure for a record close — and the McClellan Summation Index remains negative, though stabilising. Against Thursday's 244 Nasdaq new lows that is progress, but a record index with 24 new highs describes a market that has stopped falling apart rather than one that is broadly advancing.

The Russell was the exception, and it is the odd one. It fell 0.59% on a day the whole curve eased — the opposite of the mechanical relationship that put it on top of the board last Thursday — and it failed at its downward-sloping 20-day moving average. Healthcare weakness inside the index did some of it. But a small-cap index that cannot rally on lower yields is saying the problem is not only the discount rate.

Rates eased everywhere, led by the front end. Two-year −4.2bp to 4.791%, five-year −3.6 to 5.030%, ten-year −3.6 to 5.275%, thirty-year −1.7 to 5.647%, with two-to-ten at roughly 48 basis points. One session of relief after four of the opposite does not settle anything: the front end has been falling all week on the labour data and the long end has not been following it down. Tuesday it did. CPI on the 13th is the test.

Commodities kept falling on recovered supply. WTI dropped 2.68% to $87.77, its lowest of the stretch, and Brent slipped toward $100. The driver is physical rather than diplomatic: Kpler shipping data put regional crude exports above pre-conflict levels for 14 days during September, with the seven-day average at 18.3m barrels a day on 30 September. Gold rose 0.59% to $4,164.45, its first gain in three sessions, as the curve eased.

The war · the dayexports back above pre-war · crude $87.77

The flow has recovered. The stock has not.

Gulf crude exports are back above where they were before the war. Kpler shipping data shows regional exports exceeded pre-conflict levels on 14 days during September, with the seven-day moving average reaching 18.3 million barrels a day on 30 September. That is the hard number behind a fortnight of softer prices, and it is a physical fact rather than a negotiating position. WTI fell 2.68% to $87.77, the lowest of this stretch, and Brent slipped toward $100.

Set that against what the Aramco chief executive said on Monday. Amin Nasser put commercial inventory at under 6 billion barrels against roughly 10 billion when the crisis began, with less than 10% practically available, around 3 billion barrels of supply lost and over 1 billion drawn from stockpiles — and rebuilding at up to two years even after Hormuz fully reopens. Both things are true at once, and together they are the whole picture: the flow has recovered and the buffer has not. Exports at 18.3m b/d say today is comfortable. Under 10% of inventory practically available says there is nothing left to absorb a surprise.

Which makes the price a poor guide to the risk. Crude at $87.77 is the cheapest it has been since before Tehran's offer surfaced on 22 September, and on the face of it the war premium has gone. But a market running at 98% of pre-war flows with a tenth of its usable inventory is priced for continuity, not for interruption — and the diplomatic channel has produced nothing since Washington's reply reached Tehran via Qatar on 30 September. The G7's 100 million barrels, roughly a tenth of what has already been drawn, does not change that arithmetic.

For the book the practical read is unchanged. At $88 crude is neither pushing the long end up nor pulling it down, so the rate question still has to be answered by prices paid, auction supply and term premium. The asymmetry is that a renewed disruption now lands on a market with no slack, which would reprice both oil and the curve in the same direction at once. Nothing in the book is positioned for that, and nothing needs to be while the nearest stop is nine percent away.
