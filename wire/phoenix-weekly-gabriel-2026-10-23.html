Gold had the week, and it had it in the wrong direction for the story everything else was telling. Gold fell 4.14% at 2.23σ — the only two-sigma reading on the board — in a week when crude rose 5.97% to $103.27 and Brent reached its highest close since late April. An inflation hedge losing four percent while the oil price gains six is a market that has decided the energy shock is a demand problem rather than a price one. Behind gold: the Russell +2.00% at 1.33σ, the best index of the week, then crude at 0.97σ — a six percent week that is not unusual for this instrument, which is the measure of how violent its autumn has been — the S&P −0.47% at 0.44σ, the Dow +0.41% at 0.41σ and the Nasdaq −0.71% at 0.34σ.

The indices, precisely. The Russell 2000 rose 54.53 points, or 2.0%, to 2,775.90 and the Dow 212.75, or 0.4%, to 52,215.16, while the S&P fell 35.76, or 0.5%, to 7,623.68 — a second consecutive weekly loss — and the Nasdaq 184.69, or 0.7%, to 25,955.34. Small caps and blue chips up, technology down: that is a rotation week in aggregate, and it conceals four sessions that each looked completely different from the one before. For the year the board now reads Russell +11.8%, Nasdaq +11.7%, S&P +11.4%, Dow +8.6%.

Four sessions, four distinct tape shapes — that is the week's real finding. Monday: the Dow +1.02% and the Russell +1.65% both near 1.75σ while the Nasdaq printed two hundredths of a sigma, a rotation into value so clean it barely moved the index. Tuesday: nothing above a sigma. Wednesday: the Dow, the Nasdaq and the S&P all above one sigma together in a broad rally, with the Russell left behind. Thursday: the Nasdaq −2.60% at 2.43σ, its largest move in thirty sessions, with the Dow at 0.04σ. Friday: the Dow −1.23% at 2.01σ, its largest in thirty sessions, with the Nasdaq at 0.20σ. Two two-sigma days in a row, in opposite halves of the market. A board that does that is not expressing a view; it is relocating money.

The bond market is where the week's argument actually happened. It opened with French protests pushing US yields up four basis points on no American data and closed with the ten-year at 5.00%, nine basis points below the 5.09% it started from. In between: Tuesday's thirty-year fell 6bp on September housing starts, which came in at a 1.221m annual pace against 1.305m expected with single-family permits at a three-and-a-half-year low; Wednesday the long end did not move at all while France's ten-year crossed 5% for the first time since 2002; and Thursday the ten-year fell about seventeen basis points to 4.96%, its largest one-day drop since August 2025, on the AI de-rating. Friday gave four of those back. A hold at 3.75-4.00% is now 90.6% priced for Wednesday.

And France went from a story to a non-event inside five sessions. The ten-year OAT ended the week at a 24-year high, having crossed 5% on Wednesday for the first time since 2002 and passed the American ten-year on Thursday for the first time since the 2013 euro-area crisis. Carrefour fell nearly 14% on riot damage, more than 2,400 arrests were recorded, several cities imposed curfews. Then on Friday Moody's held the rating at Aa3 with a negative outlook — no downgrade — and French yields eased. Across all five sessions the honest conclusion is the one this brief reached on Tuesday and then kept re-testing: French fiscal stress is not setting the price of American debt.

What actually matteredfive sessions, filtered

The AI trade was re-priced on cash flow, not on growth

1. The week's one durable idea: the market stopped asking what the money is for and started asking where it comes from. On Wednesday Meta reported revenue up 26% to $64.60bn, raised the floor of its 2026 capital-expenditure range to $135bn and rose about 3% after hours. On Thursday a filing showed its forward commitments — mostly computing power and data-centre leases — far above July's level, and the shares fell 6.98% to $648.85. Nothing in the results had changed; the market had simply read the balance sheet instead of the income statement and noticed that $34.9bn of quarterly capex against $32.79bn of operating cash flow is negative $2.12bn of free cash flow. On Friday the same test was applied to a company with nothing to do with AI infrastructure: Bloom Energy fell 11.24%, the worst performer in the S&P, on record revenue, because the cash flow behind it was negative.

2. Which makes Wednesday the 28th the most consequential date this book has faced. The distinction the market drew on Thursday was specific: Meta funds its data centres out of advertising, while Alphabet and Microsoft also rent capacity to outside customers, so their spending is supposed to return as cloud revenue. Except that on the 21st Sundar Pichai said Google Cloud is forgoing short-term revenue to make compute trade-offs for frontier AI, called DeepMind's capacity non-negotiable and said taking new customers who need capacity next year would be incredibly difficult. He put no number on it. Alphabet reports $195-205bn of capex and a consensus near $3.00 a share on Wednesday afternoon, the same hour the Fed decides. Alphabet fell in four of the week's five sessions, including both days the Nasdaq rose.

3. The energy shock changed character twice and ended where it started. On Tuesday September housing starts fell 5.2% to a 1.221m pace against 1.305m expected, with single-family permits at a three-and-a-half-year low and the thirty-year mortgage at 7.40% — and the long end fell six basis points, pricing the oil shock as demand destruction rather than inflation. On Thursday crude rose 2.4% while the ten-year fell seventeen basis points, which said the same thing louder. Then on Friday crude rose 3.8% and the ten-year rose four, which said the opposite. September PCE on Thursday the 29th is where that argument gets an answer, and the two readings point the long end in opposite directions.

The war · the weekWTI $97.45 → $103.27 · Brent $116.72

Flows recovered, products did not, and the strikes acquired a date

The week's crude move was 5.97%, and it came in two halves. The first was diplomatic relief: on Tuesday Iran's president received Pakistan's army chief in Tehran on a new mediation proposal and crude reversed three dollars off a five-month high, and on Wednesday Russia agreed to resume US-mediated peace talks with Ukraine, possibly as early as November — read as the first credible route to easing the global diesel shortage. Then the second half: by Thursday Iran had hardened, with president Pezeshkian saying Washington cannot expect dialogue under blockade, and by Friday Brent settled $116.72, its highest close since late April, and WTI $103.27.

The physical picture improved on crude and deteriorated on product, which is the distinction that matters for inflation. Kpler put Hormuz crude flows at about 10.3m barrels a day, 76% of pre-war, with US energy secretary Chris Wright putting Gulf flows near 80% — a chokepoint running at four-fifths of capacity while being actively mined. But refined products are down to 11% of cargoes from more than 20% before the conflict, and Middle East refinery throughput was 7.3m barrels a day in August against 9.9m in February. Crude re-routes; a cracking margin does not. That reaches the consumer through diesel, freight and airfares rather than at the pump — slower, stickier, and harder to dismiss as a shock that passes.

And the war acquired a date. On Thursday the New York Times reported that the United States and Israel have drawn up plans for renewed strikes on Iran after the 3 November midterms — energy sites, nuclear facilities, missile and drone arsenals. Trump said the US is not negotiating, then accused Iran of making up fake hits on ships to drive oil higher before the vote, then said on Friday that Washington could end the war quickly. Those three statements do not reconcile. Meanwhile an attack on King Fahd International Airport at Dammam killed one person and injured eight, the IRGC Navy said four oil-carrying vessels were hit in the northern Persian Gulf and three more struck mines west of Hormuz, Aramco evacuated its Dhahran headquarters, and Kpler counted at least 18 vessels attacked in a fortnight. October is the worst month for shipping attacks in and around the Strait on Washington Institute data.

The number to carry into next week is the one with no price attached. The Department of Energy authorised an emergency exchange of up to 400,000 barrels from a Strategic Petroleum Reserve now at its lowest level since 1982, to patch a disruption at a single Louisiana refinery. Baker Hughes counted 603 oil and gas rigs, unchanged for a second week — a drilling industry declining to respond to a hundred-dollar barrel. Exxon Mobil, Chevron and ConocoPhillips all closed Friday at all-time highs. The cap on the oil price has been spent and the supply response has not arrived, which is why gold falling 4.14% in the same week is the genuinely strange reading on this page.
