The taperanked by how unusual
Sigma (σ) measures a move against that instrument’s own recent behaviour, not against zero. One sigma is a normal day. Two sigma is a move bigger than roughly 95% of its last twenty sessions — rare enough to be information rather than noise. A 1.8% day on the S&P is ordinary in a volatile month and remarkable in a calm one; sigma is what tells the difference.

Gold did something it is not supposed to do. Its 3.52% fall was 2.41σ, the largest reading on this board since crude's collapse a week ago — and it happened on the day a Middle East peace proposal was rejected and oil rose 4.22%. WTI's move was 1.25σ, the S&P's 0.77% fall 1.13σ and the Nasdaq's 0.92% 1.01σ. When bullion sells off into an escalation, the marginal buyer is not thinking about geopolitics at all — it is looking at a 5.2% ten-year, a 19-year high, and deciding that a non-yielding asset costs too much to hold.

The indices. The S&P closed 7,683.69, the Nasdaq 26,820.38, the Dow 51,481.51 and the Russell 2,817.91 — all down between 0.67% and 0.92%, with 65% of issues declining. That is a broad, orderly risk-off session rather than a rotation: nothing was rewarded for being defensive and nothing was punished for being long duration in particular.

Rates. The ten-year reached 5.2%, described as a 19-year high, and the five-year is holding above 5% after clearing it for the first time since 2007 last Wednesday. This is now a fifth consecutive session of the long end at or near fresh highs, and the driver has not changed since the PMI print: domestic activity, not inflation and not oil. That is why crude rising 4.22% did not push yields higher still — the curve is already pricing a strong economy.

Single names carried the dispersion again. Nvidia rose 2.64% on a record $150bn buyback. Boeing fell 6.2% to 2026 lows on FAA delays to the 737 Max 10, MongoDB dropped roughly 20% after its chief executive left for Meta, and Kodiak Sciences rose 154.56% on phase 3 data. A tape where the index moves 0.8% and individual names move 150% is one where the information is almost entirely idiosyncratic.

What actually matteredone session, filtered

Gold sold off into a war escalation, which tells you what is really setting prices

1. A 2.4 sigma fall in gold on the day a peace proposal collapsed is the most informative thing on the board. Trump rejected Iran's offer, oil rose 4.22%, Brent reached $108.50 — and bullion fell 3.52%. The textbook says gold rises on geopolitical risk. It did the opposite because the ten-year is at 5.2%, a 19-year high, and the cost of holding an asset that yields nothing has gone up faster than the risk premium. This is the third time in two weeks gold has fallen through an escalation. The pattern is now established, and it says the curve is the only variable that matters.

2. The Iranian offer was larger than the market understood last week. When it surfaced on 22 September it was reported as Hormuz reopened in exchange for easing military pressure and lifting the port blockade — and crude fell 8.6% on it. The terms as now reported also require the US to release frozen Iranian funds and lift sanctions. That is a materially bigger ask, it explains the rejection, and it means the gap between the two sides is wider than the price implied a week ago. Tehran says it will not soften its demands. The seven-day clock was never running.

3. The cybersecurity de-rating lasted one session, and that is a real signal. Bernstein downgraded Palo Alto, Okta and SentinelOne on Friday on the grounds the group had run roughly 100% this year; Zscaler fell 8.64% and OKTA 5.54%. On Monday OKTA rose 3.58% and took almost all of it back, on a day 65% of US issues fell. A valuation argument that is reversed within a session against a declining tape is an argument the market has heard and set aside. That does not make the multiple safe — it means the de-rating will need a demand disappointment, not an analyst note, and the next chance for one is the December quarter.

The seven-day clock was never running

Trump said on Saturday that Tehran's proposal was not acceptable. The terms, as reported, asked the United States to release frozen Iranian funds, lift sanctions and end its naval blockade; in return Iran would reopen the Strait of Hormuz and resume nuclear negotiations within seven days. That is a considerably larger request than the version that circulated on 22 September, when the offer was understood as an easing of military pressure in exchange for the strait — and when crude fell 8.6% on it in a single session.

Iran's response was to hold its position. Tehran said it will not soften its demands. Trump has since indicated he expects talks to resume this week, and a Saudi-led coalition intercepted projectiles over the weekend. So the negotiation is not dead, but the terms are far apart and the military track is still live — four days after missiles were fired at Yanbu, the terminal Aramco's pipeline bypass routes to.

Crude took back most of the week's decline. WTI rose 4.22% to $96.31 and Brent 4.02% to $108.50, with November futures at $107.35. Crude is now $3.42 above Friday's close and only $4.39 below where it sat before Tehran's offer surfaced. The entire 7.8% weekly decline has been half-undone in one session, which is what a price built on a diplomatic assumption does when the assumption is withdrawn. For the Fed it matters less than the level: at $96.31 crude is still $7.79 below where the dot plot was written.
