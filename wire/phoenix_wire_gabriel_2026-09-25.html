The taperanked by how unusual
Sigma (σ) measures a move against that instrument’s own recent behaviour, not against zero. One sigma is a normal day. Two sigma is a move bigger than roughly 95% of its last twenty sessions — rare enough to be information rather than noise. A 1.8% day on the S&P is ordinary in a volatile month and remarkable in a calm one; sigma is what tells the difference.

What actually matteredone session, filtered

A sector was de-rated two days after one of its members made its best case

1. Okta did everything right and was marked down anyway. On Wednesday it launched a consortium with eight of the largest names in enterprise computing, shipped the only generally-available product in its category, and priced it at a 25-37.5% uplift. On Friday Bernstein downgraded it alongside Palo Alto and SentinelOne on the grounds that the group had run too far too fast, and it fell 5.54%. Both things can be true: the business improved and the price had already moved. That is the same argument Bernstein made a week ago when it cut the rating while raising its target, and it is the risk of owning anything that has doubled in nine months.

2. The curve made a fourth consecutive high and equities rose into it twice. Ten-year 5.225%, highest since 2007, five-year holding five percent — and the S&P up 0.51% on Friday after being flat on Thursday. For three weeks this brief has framed a five-percent curve as the direct threat to every multiple in this book. The market is currently disagreeing, and doing so on trade optimism rather than on earnings. There is no data to settle it until 28 October, which makes the next month a test of patience rather than analysis.

3. The oil story has become a two-way negotiation and stopped being a trend. Crude fell 8.6% on Tuesday's Hormuz offer, rebounded 3.9% on Thursday's missiles at Yanbu, and eased 1.82% on Friday as truce discussion resurfaced. Across the week it is down 7.8%. For the Fed the average is what counts and the average has fallen hard: even at $92.89, crude is $11 below where it sat when twelve of eighteen members projected another hike. But a market that moves on alternate headlines is no longer pricing a supply shock — it is pricing a diplomatic process, and those can stop.

Three headlines, three directions, and a week that ended $8 lower

Friday's move was the dovish one again: WTI eased 1.82% to $92.89 and Brent 1.09% to $105.40 as discussion of a US-Iran truce resurfaced, partly unwinding Thursday's escalation bid after Houthi missiles were fired at Yanbu and Taif. The phased framework under Qatari mediation — Hormuz reopened in stages against an easing of military pressure and the port blockade — remains the live proposal.

The week in one line: $100.70 to $92.89, down 7.8%, by way of $89.42 on Tuesday and a rebound to the mid-nineties on Thursday. Hormuz traffic is still severely restricted and the East-West pipeline is still only partly restored, so nothing structural has been fixed. What has changed is that both sides are now negotiating in public, which means the price responds to statements rather than to barrels.

The transmission into this book is unchanged in direction and weaker in force. Cheaper crude still argues against the Fed's projected hike — and this week's PMI and a 5.2% ten-year argue for it more loudly. Energy is no longer the binding input on the rate path; domestic activity is. Watch the October CPI for how much of the $11 decline actually reaches the print.
