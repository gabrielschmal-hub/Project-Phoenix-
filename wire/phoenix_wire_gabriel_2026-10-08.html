The taperanked by how unusual
Sigma (σ) measures a move against that instrument’s own recent behaviour, not against zero. One sigma is a normal day. Two sigma is a move bigger than roughly 95% of its last twenty sessions — rare enough to be information rather than noise. A 1.8% day on the S&P is ordinary in a volatile month and remarkable in a calm one; sigma is what tells the difference.

What hit technology was a funding question, not an earnings one. The 29,000 payroll print six days ago argued the opposite. CPI on Tuesday is the only thing that can arbitrate.

Oil rose again and the reason changed twice in a day. WTI settled $91.49, up 1.52%, after reaching almost $93, with Brent above $104. Two forces pulled against each other: Iranian attacks on tankers in the Strait of Hormuz and about a quarter of Gulf of Mexico output shut in for Hurricane Isaias pushing up, against Trump's statement that the United States will not strike Iran before the November midterms pulling down. What actually matteredone session, filtered

A sector event inside a market that stayed still

1. The AI trade sold off on its own, which has not happened before in this stretch. The Nasdaq fell 1.25% while the Dow rose 0.10%, the Russell 0.03% and most S&P constituents were higher. For three weeks every index move in these pages has been the same handful of large technology names carrying or dragging everything; Thursday they moved alone. The trigger was specific and it was about funding, not demand: an Nvidia-backed Australian data-centre company failed to raise the support it sought. That is a question about the capital stack behind AI capacity rather than about whether anyone wants the compute, and it is the more dangerous of the two questions, because capacity already contracted has to be paid for either way. Alphabet has $195-205bn of guided capex and, since Tuesday, a twenty-year obligation for 3.6 gigawatts of power.

2. The thirty-year auction was the supply thesis's first real test, and yields fell. For a fortnight this brief has argued that the long end rising on data that argued for lower yields could only be explained by bond supply and term premium. An auction is where that claim is settled: it is the moment the market has to absorb actual paper. Thursday's thirty-year auction came with the ten-year reaching toward 5.35% in the morning, and the ten-year closed near 5.24% — the lowest in a week. That is one data point against the thesis, and it deserves recording as plainly as the five that supported it. It does not settle the question: the thirty-year is still near a 24-year high, the Fed's minutes still promise a hike, and France's projected interest bill still exceeds its education budget. But the first auction since the argument began did not go badly.

3. Two labour readings six days apart cannot both be describing the economy. On 2 October payrolls came in at 29,000 against 90,000 expected, with 60,000 revised out of July and August and July turned negative. On Thursday initial claims came in at 197,000 against 200,000 expected, the lowest since July and near a 57-year low for a fourth consecutive week. Hiring has stopped and firing has not started — which is a labour market that is frozen rather than weakening, and a frozen one gives the Fed no reason to cut and no reason to panic. That is the reading most consistent with Waller saying more hikes will likely be needed, and with a market that has priced a 78% hold in October while the committee's own minutes say otherwise. CPI on Tuesday breaks the tie.

The war · the dayno strike before the midterms · crude $91.49

Trump ruled out an attack before November, and tankers were hit anyway

Trump said on Thursday that the United States will not attack Iran before the November midterm elections, citing productive diplomacy with Tehran. That is the first dated, specific commitment on timing since the campaign began, and it puts a floor under the calendar: whatever the military track does, a US strike is off the table for roughly four weeks. It follows the reply that reached Tehran through Qatari mediation on 30 September, whose contents neither side has disclosed.

And on the same day, Iranian attacks on tankers in the Strait of Hormuz. The two facts are not contradictory — they are the pattern of the past fortnight at its clearest: a negotiating channel that keeps functioning and a military situation that keeps producing incidents. What is new is that Washington has now said it will not respond with force on a stated timetable, which removes the escalation that the oil price has been intermittently pricing since late September.

Crude went up anyway, for a different reason. WTI settled $91.49, up 1.52%, after reaching almost $93 intraday, with Brent above $104. The pull came from Hurricane Isaias, with about a quarter of Gulf of Mexico output shut in and landfall forecast for the weekend, and from the tanker attacks; the push back came from Trump's statement. Oil ending higher on a day the United States ruled out a strike tells you the marginal barrel is now a physical problem rather than a geopolitical one.

Which is where the inventory arithmetic bites. Aramco's Amin Nasser put commercial stocks at under 6 billion barrels against roughly 10 billion before the crisis, with less than 10% practically available. A market with no buffer reprices on weather, and did. The asymmetry for this book is unchanged: nothing here is positioned for an oil shock, nothing needs to be while the nearest stop is more than ten percent away, and a renewed supply interruption would move crude and the curve in the same direction at once.
