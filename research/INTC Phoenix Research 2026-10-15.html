The business in depth

The original x86 chipmaker, mid-way through a costly attempt to become a contract manufacturer for other chip designers — now with the US government and Nvidia as shareholders.

What it sells

Client Computing — Core Ultra processors for PCs, still the largest revenue line. Data Center and AI — Xeon server CPUs and Gaudi accelerators, losing share to AMD and Arm-based chips. Intel Foundry — manufacturing on its 18A and future 14A processes for Intel and outside customers; deeply loss-making while it scales. Plus stakes in Mobileye (driver assistance) and Altera (FPGAs, majority sold).

Who buys it — customers

PC makers Dell, HP, Lenovo; server makers and hyperscalers; for the foundry, external design customers that are still few — the US government (secure chips), and partners such as Microsoft and Amazon. Nvidia invested $5bn in 2025 to co-develop x86 chips with its GPUs, making it both a shareholder and a prospective customer.

Who it depends on — suppliers

Equipment from ASML (including High-NA EUV), Applied Materials, Lam Research, Tokyo Electron. Ironically, TSMC manufactures some of Intel's own chip tiles. Capital: the US government (CHIPS grants converted to a ~10% stake), Nvidia, SoftBank, and the $22.6bn August 2026 equity offering at $95.

Who it fights — competitors

CPUs: AMD, plus Arm-based processors from Apple, Qualcomm, Nvidia and the hyperscalers' own designs. Foundry: TSMC (dominant) and Samsung. AI: Nvidia and AMD.

Why this matters for the tradeIntel's equity story is foundry execution: 18A yields and landing outside customers. The $95 offering price matters for the chart because ~242m shares were bought there — the price is now sitting on it.
