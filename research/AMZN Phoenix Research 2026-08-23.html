The business

A retail logistics network that happens to own the largest cloud on earth — and the cloud is where the money is.

Amazon runs three businesses that look unrelated and are not. Retail generates the traffic, advertising monetises it at near-pure margin, and AWS earns the majority of operating income. The AI cycle is currently accelerating the third while the first two fund it.

AWS — the profit engine

$42.2bn, +37% — fastest growth in 18 quarters. Operating income $16.6bn, +63%, at a 39.4% margin, up 650bps year over year.

$169bn annualised run rate and a $496bn backlog growing triple digits. That backlog is contracted work not yet delivered — the closest thing to visibility any hyperscaler publishes.

Stores — the traffic

North America $116.2bn, +16%; International $42.2bn, +15%. Over 40% more items delivered same-day or overnight in the first half.

Grocery and everyday essentials growing faster than the rest; perishable customers up 50% since January, same-day perishables in 2,300 US cities.

Advertising — the quiet margin

$19.8bn, +26%. Sold against demand the retail business already owns, so incremental cost is close to zero.

Now larger than most standalone media companies and growing faster than the group. This is where operating leverage actually shows up.

Every line beat, and the market believed it. Alphabet grew Cloud 82% and fell; Meta beat and fell nearly 10%; Amazon beat and rose 9.5%. The difference was credibility — two years of promising the AI spend would pay, then showing it.

What to discount

Net income of $62.6bn is not what it looks like. It includes a $53.4bn non-operating pre-tax gain, largely a valuation adjustment on the Anthropic stake. Operating income of $27.5bn is the real number.

FY26 capex guide$220bn, up from $200bn
TTM property purchases$169bn · +64%
TTM free cash flow−$7.6bn (was +$18.2bn)
Tariff refund in Q2$600m one-time

Same pattern as Alphabet: free cash flow has swung negative to fund AI capacity. Amazon absorbed tariff costs rather than passing them on.

The line that separates this from the othersJassy said Amazon will not have enough capacity to meet demand in 2026 and 2027. That reframes the capex debate entirely: Alphabet's spending is a bet that demand will arrive; Amazon's is a response to demand it is already turning away. A $496bn backlog growing triple digits is the evidence — and it is why the same negative free cash flow that punished GOOGL was rewarded here.
Read the Q3 guide carefullyGuidance of $197–202bn implies 9–12% growth against Q2's 20%, and came in below the $204bn analysts modelled. The cause is a calendar artefact: Prime Day moved from July to June, pulling spend into Q2. Excluding it, Amazon says Q3 growth would be nearly 400bps higher. The deceleration is arithmetic, not demand — but it will read as a slowdown in October if the adjustment is forgotten.

Reading the table: Stop dist is how far the stop sits from entry as a percentage of the share price — it is not the percentage of the account at risk. Account risk is 1.00% in every row, fixed at $20 on a $2,000 book. Share count is derived from it: shares = $20 ÷ (entry − stop). A wider stop buys fewer shares, not more risk. The only figure that varies with stop distance is position value, which is why the concentration cap and the 1R budget are separate constraints on separate axes.

The trap in row one

A stop at $254 gives a headline 4.62:1 — and sits 0.61 ATR from entry, inside a single day's noise, in negative-gamma territory.

That ratio is manufactured by a stop that will be hit. Ignore it.

For 2 : 1

The pullback entry near $257 with a stop under $250 gives 2.56:1 to the OI wall and 3.36:1 to the old high, on a sane 1.18 ATR stop.

Best risk-adjusted entry in the series.
