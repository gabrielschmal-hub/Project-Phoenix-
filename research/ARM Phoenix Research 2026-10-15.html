The business in depth

It designs the processor architecture inside almost every smartphone and a growing share of servers and PCs — and is paid by everyone else who makes the chips.

What it sells

Arm does not (yet) sell chips at scale. It sells licences to its instruction set and processor designs (Cortex CPUs, Neoverse server cores, Mali GPUs), and collects a royalty on every chip shipped — typically a few percent of chip price. Newer Compute Subsystems sell more complete designs at higher royalty rates, and Arm has begun developing its own chips. SoftBank owns about 90% of the shares.

Who buys it — customers

Apple, Qualcomm, Samsung, MediaTek in phones; Nvidia (Grace CPUs); the hyperscalers' own server chips — Amazon Graviton, Google Axion, Microsoft Cobalt; automotive and IoT chipmakers. Over 300bn Arm-based chips have shipped.

Who it depends on — suppliers

As an IP company its inputs are engineers and chip-design software from Synopsys and Cadence. For its own silicon it would depend on TSMC.

Who it fights — competitors

x86 from Intel and AMD in PCs and servers; the open-source RISC-V architecture (SiFive and others), which is royalty-free and gaining in embedded and China. Its move into chips risks turning customers — above all Qualcomm, with which it is in litigation — into competitors.

Why this matters for the tradeArm's valuation is among the highest of any large chip company because royalties scale without capital. That leaves it exposed to any sign of slower royalty growth and to the Qualcomm case. Earnings 4 November.
