The business in depth

The world's most valuable carmaker, valued mostly for businesses that are not yet large: robotaxis, humanoid robots and energy storage.

What it sells

Automotive is still most of revenue: Model 3 and Model Y (the bulk of volume), Cybertruck, Model S and X, plus regulatory credits sold to other carmakers and FSD (driver-assistance) software. Energy Generation and Storage — Megapack grid batteries and Powerwall — is the fastest-growing and increasingly profitable segment. Services: insurance, Supercharging, repairs. Development projects: the Robotaxi service and Optimus robots.

Who buys it — customers

Consumers in the US, China and Europe; fleets and rental companies; utilities and developers for Megapack; other automakers who pay to use the Supercharger network and buy regulatory credits.

Who it depends on — suppliers

Batteries from CATL, LG Energy Solution and Panasonic, alongside Tesla's own 4680 cells; lithium and other materials under long-term contracts. AI chips: its own inference chips made by Samsung and TSMC, and Nvidia GPUs for training. Thousands of parts suppliers; China's Shanghai factory is both a supplier hub and the main export base.

Who it fights — competitors

EVs: BYD (larger by volume), Geely, Xiaomi and other Chinese makers, plus Volkswagen, Hyundai and GM. Autonomy: Waymo (already operating driverless at scale), Zoox, and Chinese robotaxi firms. Storage: Fluence, Sungrow, BYD. Robots: Figure, Unitree and others.

Why this matters for the tradeCar margins have been compressed by price cuts and competition, so the valuation rests on autonomy and robots — dated by Musk's targets, not by financial results. That makes the stock highly sensitive to each earnings call's narrative. Wednesday 21 October is that call.

What the chart shows

From April to July Tesla held a range of roughly $380 to $450. The July earnings week took it from $381 to $313 — an 18% drop — with a low of $297. It has since recovered to $388, almost exactly the old range floor, and turned down again this week.

The pattern call — and what to do with it

Bulkowski's dead-cat bounce: an event drops the stock 15% or more, it bounces, and then frequently resumes falling. His data: another large drop has a 26% chance within three months and 38% within six; his rule is to avoid the stock for at least six months. Wednesday's print falls at the three-month mark. Separately, the recovery has stalled at $380–388, where the old floor now acts as resistance. Nothing in the chart argues for an entry before the print.
