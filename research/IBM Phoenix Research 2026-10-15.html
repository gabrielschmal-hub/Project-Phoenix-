The business in depth

A 115-year-old company now built around hybrid-cloud software (Red Hat), consulting, and the mainframes that still run most of the world's bank transactions.

What it sells

Software (~45% of revenue): Red Hat (Linux, OpenShift), automation (including HashiCorp), data and AI (watsonx), security, and transaction-processing software tied to the mainframe. Consulting (~30%): technology and business-transformation services. Infrastructure (~25%): IBM Z mainframes, Power servers and storage — cyclical with each new mainframe generation. Plus financing.

Who buys it — customers

Large enterprises and governments — above all banks, insurers and airlines that run core systems on mainframes. Most of the Fortune 500 use Red Hat. Consulting clients overlap.

Who it depends on — suppliers

Samsung Foundry manufactures IBM's Z and Power processors; component and memory suppliers for hardware. In consulting, its supply is people — and partners such as Microsoft, AWS, SAP and Salesforce whose software it implements.

Who it fights — competitors

Software: Microsoft, Oracle, Broadcom/VMware, ServiceNow. Consulting: Accenture, Deloitte, Infosys, TCS. Infrastructure: Dell, HPE, and the public clouds (AWS, Azure, Google) that compete for the same workloads.

Why this matters for the tradeThe July print exposed that mainframe and consulting timing can swing a quarter badly — signings slipped and the Z cycle slowed, and a securities investigation followed. Software growth is the stabiliser. Wednesday's print tests whether those delayed deals closed.

What the chart shows

IBM spiked from $219 to $332 in June, faded to $287 by mid-July, then fell 25% in one session on earnings to $212, with a low of $199. Since August it has traded in a box between roughly $218 and $252, closing at $222.64 below the floor once in late September before recovering.

The pattern call — and what to do with it

The July session is a dead-cat bounce event in Bulkowski's sense: a 15%+ drop on news. His rule is to avoid the stock for at least six months — until mid-January — because a further large drop has a 26% chance within three months and 38% within six. Wednesday's print falls inside that window. The box since August is a rectangle; Bulkowski notes busted rectangle bottoms (a close below the floor that quickly reverses back above the top) work well — but the late-September dip below $225 recovered only to the middle, not the top.
