The business in depth

Two companies in one: the leading designer of custom AI chips and data-centre networking silicon, and — after VMware — one of the largest enterprise software vendors.

What it sells

Semiconductor Solutions (~60%): custom AI accelerators ("XPUs") co-designed for hyperscalers — most notably Google's TPU; Ethernet switching and routing chips (Tomahawk, Jericho) that wire AI clusters together; wireless RF parts for smartphones; broadband, storage and industrial chips. Infrastructure Software (~40%): VMware virtualisation, plus CA and Symantec enterprise products, increasingly sold as bundled subscriptions.

Who buys it — customers

A short list of very large customers. Google is the anchor for custom silicon, with Meta, ByteDance and OpenAI among other XPU customers. Apple buys RF and wireless components. Network-equipment makers and hyperscalers buy switching chips. VMware's customers are large enterprises and governments — many unhappy about the post-acquisition price increases.

Who it depends on — suppliers

Fabless: TSMC manufactures its leading chips and packages the AI accelerators; HBM for XPUs comes from SK Hynix, Samsung and Micron; substrates and test from Asian suppliers.

Who it fights — competitors

Custom AI chips: Marvell, MediaTek, Alchip and GUC — and Nvidia, whose GPUs are the alternative to a custom chip. Networking: Nvidia (Spectrum-X, InfiniBand). RF: Qualcomm, Skyworks, Qorvo. Software: Nutanix, Red Hat, Microsoft Hyper-V, and customers moving workloads to public cloud.

Why this matters for the tradeThe custom-silicon thesis — hyperscalers designing their own AI chips with Broadcom rather than buying Nvidia — is what drove the stock to $495. Customer concentration is the risk: a change in Google's or Meta's chip roadmap moves the whole segment. The $495 spike reversed in the same week, and the next earnings are not until December.
