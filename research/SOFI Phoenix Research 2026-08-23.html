The business

What it sells, who pays for it, and how it got from a student-loan refinancer to a chartered bank.

SoFi runs a digital bank and, underneath it, the payments infrastructure that other banks and consumer brands rent. Those are two different businesses with two different customers, and the second one is the part most people miss.

Lending — the engine

Personal loans for debt consolidation, student-loan refinancing, and home loans including HELOC. $724.8m of Q2 net revenue and $14.8bn of originations in the quarter.

Customer: prime and near-prime US consumers, historically high-income early-career professionals. Supplier: its own depositors — and that is the whole point of the charter.

Financial Services — the flywheel

Checking and savings, investing, credit card, insurance, SoFi Plus subscription, and the SoFiUSD stablecoin. $466m of Q2 revenue, up 29%.

Mostly fee and interchange income. Its job is not margin — it is giving a borrower five more reasons to stay, which is what drives the cross-buy number.

Technology Platform — the hidden business

Galileo (card issuing and payments processing) plus Technisys (cloud core banking), now sold as SoFi Tech Solutions. $85m in Q2, up 13% sequentially.

Customer: other fintechs, banks and consumer brands — roughly 158 million enabled accounts. B2B revenue, no credit risk, and it powers SoFi's own stack.

How it got here

2020 — acquires Galileopayments infrastructure
2021 — lists via SPAC2.6m members
2022 — buys Golden Pacific Bancorpnational bank charter
2022 — acquires Technisyscloud core banking
2024 — first full GAAP-profitable yearrecord revenue

The charter is the pivot. Before 2022 SoFi borrowed wholesale to lend; after it, deposits fund the book. Deposits now exceed 90% of average total liabilities and cost of funds fell 36bps year over year — that single change is why net interest margin can expand while originations hit records.

The bottleneck, and why it is crossedA lender's binding constraint is funding. Warehouse lines are expensive and revocable; insured member deposits are cheap and sticky. Deposits grew $5.3bn in one quarter to $45.5bn and now fund over 90% of the balance sheet. The evidence it is working is the margin: NIM expanded to 5.98% on a 36bp fall in cost of funds while originations hit a record. A lender widening margin while growing volume is not competing for money.
Where the credit actually sitsThe vintage disclosure is the most useful number management publishes. Loans originated Q4 2022 to Q3 2025 show 4.68% net cumulative losses with 35% of principal still outstanding, against 6.43% at the same point for the 2017 vintage — the last cohort that approached the 7-8% tolerance. For the full 2020-2026 book to reach 8%, the remaining 38% of principal would have to charge off at roughly 10%, far above anything observed. Credit is the thing that kills lenders, and on the disclosed data it is improving rather than deteriorating.

Reading the walls correctlyA call wall is resistance, not a ceiling. Dealer selling into it is finite; real buying is not. Cleared on volume, the mechanic inverts — dealers short gamma above the strike must buy to re-hedge, and the open interest that capped the move begins fuelling it. Once through, the wall becomes support on the way back, exactly as $18 did. The correct reading is not "capped at 20" but "20 is where it gets hard, and clearing it on volume makes the next leg easier." Holding that optionality requires a trailing exit rather than a fixed target — section 05.

Reading the table: Stop dist is how far the stop sits from entry as a percentage of the share price — it is not the percentage of the account at risk. Account risk is 1.00% in every row, fixed at $20 on a $2,000 book. Share count is derived from it: shares = $20 ÷ (entry − stop). A wider stop buys fewer shares, not more risk. The only figure that varies with stop distance is position value, which is why the concentration cap and the 1R budget are separate constraints on separate axes.
