The business

A three-sided marketplace that has become a cash generator, now spending that cash to own the autonomous layer.

Uber matches demand to supply and takes a cut. What changed is that the network reached the size where each incremental trip costs almost nothing to serve — and the company is now using the resulting cash flow to buy a position in a technology that could eliminate its largest cost line.

Mobility — the profit engine

Ride-hailing across ~70 countries. 300 million weekly trips. Operating income margin 7.6% of gross bookings.

Customer: riders. Supplier: ~8m drivers, who are also the largest cost — which is the whole reason autonomy matters.

Delivery — the scale play

Uber Eats plus grocery and retail. Low margin by design, high frequency, and the reason a rider becomes a weekly user rather than a monthly one.

Supplier: restaurants and couriers. Under real pressure in Brazil, where food-delivery competition is bidding up two-wheeler supply.

Freight + Advertising

Freight brokerage is small and cyclical. Advertising is the quiet one — near-pure margin sold against demand the platform already owns.

This is where operating leverage actually shows up: revenue with no incremental driver or courier cost attached.

Fourth consecutive quarter of 20%+ bookings growth, and earnings growing faster than revenue at every line. The revenue miss was $30m on $14.2bn — a rounding error that the market treated as a signal.

The take-rate optical illusion

Mobility take rate fell about 500bps, which reads alarming. Roughly 400bps of it is a UK business-model change that alters how revenue is recognised, not what Uber earns.

Underlying net take rate is broadly stable and mobility operating margin held at 7.6%. Read gross bookings and operating income, not revenue and take rate, for the next several quarters.

The autonomous bet — what has actually been committed

Scale and money

  • $10bn+ multi-year programme — equity stakes in partners plus balance-sheet support for fleets, real estate and vehicle off-take
  • 120,000 vehicle off-take commitments
  • Every $1 Uber invests has catalysed $2.50 of outside capital into partners
  • Live in 7 cities today → targeting 15 by year-end
  • AV is still under 0.5% of 300m weekly trips

The partner stack

  • Driving tech: Waymo, Zoox, Wayve, Baidu, Pony.ai, Nuro
  • Hardware: Lucid, Nissan, Verne, Volkswagen, NVIDIA
  • Launching: Nuro+Lucid in the Bay, Zoox in Vegas, Wayve in London and Tokyo, Baidu in London, Pony/Verne in Zagreb
  • Waymo exclusivity in Austin and Atlanta ends by early 2028; the Phoenix pilot already wound down

The strategy is deliberately non-exclusive. Management said plainly they do not want dependence on one partner. That is the correct posture and it is also why the Waymo headlines matter less than they read — over 20 AV partnerships globally, and the thesis is to be the commercialisation layer rather than the technology owner.

Drone delivery — announced 17 August, four days before this data

The Zipline partnership

  • Strategic partnership plus an equity investment in Zipline, amount undisclosed
  • Target: one million drone deliveries per day by end-2029
  • First deployments this year in Dallas and Houston, then "dozens of US cities"
  • Zipline delivers every 20 seconds across four continents; US average click-to-delivery ~18 minutes, best case 5
  • First time Zipline has been fully integrated into a retailer's app

It is not the first drone deal

  • Flytrex — September 2025, equity stake, operating in Dallas. One of only four FAA-authorised beyond-visual-line-of-sight providers
  • Manna — Ireland
  • Sidewalk robots alongside couriers
  • Over 30 autonomous partners across mobility, delivery and freight

The pattern is consistent and it is the actual strategy. Uber does not build the autonomy — it takes equity stakes in the people who do, then routes demand through them. Ground AV, aerial drones and sidewalk robots are the same playbook: own the demand layer, rent the technology. Khosrowshahi described customer appetite for rapid delivery as "insatiable".

On "little competition" — the honest readDrone delivery is a genuinely new revenue line and Uber is moving fast into it. But it is not an uncontested market. DoorDash is building an in-house drone programme and has just secured a key FAA certification. Amazon and Walmart already run drone delivery in selected markets — and Walmart is a Zipline customer, which makes it partner and competitor at once.

What Uber genuinely has: the demand layer. Millions of consumers and hundreds of thousands of merchants already on the platform. DoorDash must build drones and keep its marketplace; Amazon and Walmart have logistics but a narrower merchant set. Uber can route a delivery to a courier, a robot or a drone by cost and distance — the hybrid network is the moat, not the aircraft.

What the record cash flow buys: $10bn TTM free cash flow funds equity stakes across every autonomy vertical simultaneously — which no competitor can match. That is a real structural advantage. But it is capital deployed against a 2029 target with no disclosed P&L path, which is the same criticism the AV programme attracts, and it lands on the same line of the income statement.

Delivery Hero — the other big commitmentAgreed acquisition expanding the platform to nearly 100 markets and roughly doubling the number where Uber offers both mobility and delivery. Timeline is long: close in H2 2027, planning through 2028, primary technology migration in 2029. Synergies are platform consolidation and shared infrastructure. Nothing in the next eighteen months depends on it, and integration risk is a 2029 problem — but the capital is committed now.
Where the operating leverage comes fromAI is showing up in the P&L rather than the press release: Cart Builder is lifting average order sizes by up to 2×, and AI coding tools have doubled code output per engineer. That is why non-GAAP operating income grew 40% against bookings at 22%.

Reward-to-risk

The wall is 1.5% away. That is the problem with this setup, and it is arithmetic rather than opinion.

The structure is unusually hostile to a long here. Price sits at $78.80 with the call wall at $80 and the gamma flip at $76.19. There is 1.5% of room above and 3.4% of air below before dealer hedging inverts. Any stop that clears the flip is more than a full ATR wide, and the nearest meaningful target is only 1.5% away.

Reading the table: Stop dist is how far the stop sits from entry as a percentage of the share price — it is not the percentage of the account at risk. Account risk is 1.00% in every row, fixed at $20 on a $2,000 book. Share count is derived from it: shares = $20 ÷ (entry − stop). A wider stop buys fewer shares, not more risk. The only figure that varies with stop distance is position value, which is why the concentration cap and the 1R budget are separate constraints on separate axes.

Buying here does not work

At $78.80 the only stop that clears the gamma flip is $74.82 — 5.1% and 1.45 ATR. Against a wall 1.5% overhead, that is 0.29:1 to the first target.

Do not enter into a wall.

For 2 : 1

Requires either a breakout entry above $80.30 targeting $90, or a pullback entry near the flip at $76.50. Both work; neither is available at Friday's close.

Wait for one of the two.

For 3 : 1

Only the pullback entry delivers it: $76.50 with a stop at $73.76 gives 3.10:1 to $85 and 4.93:1 to $90, on a 1.00 ATR stop.

Cleanest structure of the three names.

If $80 breaks — the case against a fixed targetDealer selling into 42,633 contracts is finite. Cleared on volume, the same open interest reverses into buying and $80 becomes support. With balanced 1.53:1 call-to-put open interest there is no crowded position to unwind, so a break would be real demand rather than a hedging squeeze — which makes it more durable, not less. Entry 80.30 with a stop at 76.19 pays 1.14:1 at $85, 2.36:1 at $90, 4.79:1 at $100. A fixed target at $85 would exit at the worst possible moment.
Cost floor. €1 per round trip on a $2,000 account is 0.054R. The book needs 1.70R to break even after commission. The pullback scenario clears it comfortably; buying at the wall does not clear it at all.

The answer

A business that has crossed into serious cash generation — $10bn trailing free cash flow, operating income growing at nearly twice the rate of bookings, and a deliberate, well-capitalised position across ground autonomy, sidewalk robots and now drones. It scores 72/100 and it is not a trade for this book: stage2 fails on a falling 30-week average and near_high misses by 15 percentage points.

The structural problem is immediate rather than distant. The $80 call wall sits 1.5% overhead and Friday's high was $80.22. Buying here means entering directly into 42,633 contracts of dealer supply with a stop that must be a full ATR wide to clear the gamma flip. 0.29:1.

Two entries work and neither exists yet: a close above $80.30 (2.36:1 to $90), or a pullback to $76.50 with a stop under the flip (3.10:1 to $85, 4.93:1 to $90). The pullback is the cleanest reward-to-risk of the three names reviewed this weekend.
